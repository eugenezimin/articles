# The Cost of Concurrency and Parallelism

The opening article of a benchmarking series that questions the habit of reaching for threads by default. Most concurrency benchmarks run under ideal conditions, with large, isolated, well-partitioned workloads where the overhead of parallelism is invisible. When the units of work are tiny, however, the cost of spawning a thread, queuing a job, and waking a worker can make the concurrent version slower than the sequential one. The goal is to quantify that **pure dispatch overhead** across raw thread creation and join, thread pools with work-stealing and lock-based queues, and higher-level abstractions such as `async`, and to draw a **decision boundary** for when concurrency stops paying off.

The methodology is laid out in detail. The payload is a fixed, side-effect-free CPU workload, ten thousand terms of the Leibniz series for π, which finishes in microseconds. One sample is a fan-out of a hundred such tasks, with a warm-up run followed by a thousand timed samples per variant. Results are reported as **p50**, **p99**, and **p999** rather than a mean, because dispatch cost is a distribution whose tail reveals scheduler hiccups, GC pauses, and cold pools. Each task's result is folded into a global XOR accumulator so the optimizer cannot delete the work and no printing happens inside the timed region. The article is candid about what it cannot control on Apple silicon, where clocks can't be pinned and tasks migrate between core types, and it documents the test machine (Apple M5 Pro) and the pinned runtime versions of Rust, Go, Node.js, Python (including the free-threading build), and Java.

It closes by explaining why Rust comes first: being ahead-of-time compiled, with no garbage collector or interpreter, it provides the cleanest baseline before the same measurements are repeated for Go's scheduler, Java's JIT and GC tail, Python's interpreter, and Node's runtime.
