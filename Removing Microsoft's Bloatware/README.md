# What Microsoft leaves behind on a Mac, and how to remove all of it

An article about why dragging Microsoft software to the Trash is not an uninstall, and about the script written to do the job properly. It opens with Microsoft AutoUpdate offering updates for apps that had already been deleted, and shows on a real Mac why that happens: a launch agent that runs at login and every two hours, a launch daemon, and a privileged helper that runs as root, all in `/Library` and outside every app bundle.

The article follows `ms-uninstall.sh` in the order it works. Apps are found by **bundle identifier rather than name**, because Windows App, Teams and OneDrive keep getting renamed. One identifier is then **mapped to roughly two dozen locations** in `~/Library` and `/Library`, grouped into data, caches, and things that run. Products are expanded into their **sibling identifiers**, since OneDrive alone has seven and Defender six. The central idea is the **keeper's veto**: every shared component, such as AutoUpdate, the `UBF8T346G9` group containers that hold Outlook's mail store, and the Office fonts, declares which apps still need it, and any app you keep blocks its removal. Further sections cover reading the dry-run plan, granting Full Disk Access, the **order of operations** (quit, `launchctl bootout`, move to Trash, restart `cfprefsd`) with the failure each step prevents, and the guard rails around `rm -rf`. It closes with what the script leaves behind, including package receipts, a Teams audio driver, Edge's profile, and a warning about managed Macs.

Ten figures accompany the text: five Ghostty terminal captures of real dry runs on the author's Mac and five diagrams. They are rebuilt with `tools/capture.sh` (read-only, dry runs only) followed by `tools/render.sh`, with sources in `tools/src/`. The script lives in the [development-utilities](https://github.com/eugenezimin/development-utilities/tree/main/ms-uninstall) repository.
